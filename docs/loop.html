SmartClock Disciplining Loop

How the receiver steers its 10811 oscillator to GPS, as read from the firmware images: a proportional–integral loop on the ten-second mean of the phase error, with the oscillator's own aging curve fed forward into the integrator. Every constant below is one the code contains.

The loop

Once a second the firmware reads one interval from the FPGA counter. Every tenth second it takes the mean x̄ of the ten readings — the number :SYNChronization:TINTerval? reports — and runs one update:

e = x̄ − x₀ x₀ is a setpoint, normally 0 f ← (1 − a)·f + a·e a = 29.75 / τ d = clamp(b + c / y, −M, +M) y = t / 2700 s, from the aging fit I ← I + 10·k·f + 10·d / 2700 k = 1 / (4·G·τ²) u = K·f + B + I + c₀·s K = 1 / (G·τ)

u is the EFC word sent to the DAC. B is the EFC that was in force when the loop closed, less c₀·s, so closing the loop does not move the oscillator; s is the health monitor's exponential average of the oscillator's oven current (0.1 of each fresh reading, 0.9 of the last), and c₀ is the value :DIAGnostic:ROSCillator:TCOefficient reads and writes — a calibration constant in the EEPROM block, in EFC units per unit of the current channel, applied at every update and adjusted by nothing but its own setter. That term tracks the oven rather than the phase; the console word xcal measures it by fitting EFC against current.

What kind of loop that is

Take the oscillator as an integrator: an EFC change of Δu shifts its fractional frequency by G·Δu, and frequency integrates into phase. With the proportional gain K = 1/(Gτ) and an integral gain of k per second, the closed loop is second order:

s² + (G·K)·s + G·k = s² + s/τ + 1/(4τ²) = (s + 1/(2τ))²

A double real pole at −1/(2τ): natural frequency 1/(2τ), damping ratio exactly 1. The loop's own settling time is 2τ — about 1000 s on the Z3816A and 2000 s on the Z3801A — and it does not overshoot.

Two things the continuous form leaves out:

The prefilter on the error is a first-order low-pass with time constant 10 s / a = τ / 2.975, about 168 s at τ = 500 s. It adds a third real pole roughly six times faster than the loop's pair.

The update is discrete at 10 s, which is short against 2τ, so the continuous approximation holds; the factor 10 on the integrator term is the sample period.

G cancels out of the closed-loop dynamics: it only scales the gains so the poles land at −1/(2τ) whatever the oscillator's actual sensitivity. If the real oscillator gain differs from the assumed G, the poles move by that ratio.

The aging feed-forward

The dashed path is what makes the receiver's holdover good. While the loop is closed, pllp averages the current-compensated EFC, u − c₀·s, over each 2700 s (45 min) window and pushes one sample into a ring of 64 — 48 hours. The curv task fits

EFC(t) = a + b·y + c·ln y y = t / 2700 s, t the seconds since cold start

to those samples (a least-squares line, plus a one-dimensional search on c once there are enough points). The slope of that curve now, b + c/y, is d: the EFC change per 2700 s the fit predicts. Each update the integrator advances by 10·d/2700 — the predicted change over ten seconds — before the loop sees any phase error from it. In holdover the same curve keeps advancing the EFC with nothing to correct it; startup_pll also takes its first EFC from it after a restart.

Samples are weighted by how much of the window was averaged: 3 if all 2700 seconds, 2 if at least 675, else 1; and everything in the first three hours after a cold start gets weight 1. A fit needs at least three samples, so d is zero for the first hours after a power-up.

Starting up

Before pll_normal, startup_pll runs the same update law with its own time constant, recomputing K, k and a from it every update. It first waits for sixteen consecutive ten-second means within ±150 ns, then lengthens its constant from 150 s by 5 s per update until it is within 5 s of τ, sets it to τ, and hands over. The Z3801A's firmware does the same.
